**Prepared:** September 10, 2026
**Rates and limits retrieved:** September 10, 2026
**Scope:** Bucks and Montgomery County, Pennsylvania | FHA 30-year fixed financing

---

## 1. The 60-second version

- **Your instinct about Upper Black Eddy is half right.** It has the **lowest property
  tax rate of the three by far** (0.98% vs. 1.36% Harleysville, 1.45% Collegeville) —
  worth ~$110–$130/month on a $300k–$350k home. But it is **not price-cheap**: the
  ZIP 18972 median is about **$562,000**, and almost everything below $250,000 is
  vacant land rather than housing. Its realistic entry point is ~$300,000.
- **Harleysville has the best affordable selection** — six listings under $400,000
  versus three in Upper Black Eddy (two of which are land) and one in Collegeville.
  At entry-level prices, Harleysville and Upper Black Eddy cost within ~$94/month
  of each other.
- **The FHA limit is not your constraint.** Bucks and Montgomery both sit at **$630,200**
  for a 1-unit home in 2026 — the highest tier in Pennsylvania. Your budget is limited
  by income and cash, not by the loan cap.
- **Budget ~$2,770/month all-in for a $350,000 home in Harleysville**, or **~$2,662**
  in Upper Black Eddy. That is principal, interest, FHA mortgage insurance, taxes,
  and insurance combined.
- **Closing costs are beatable.** Plan on ~$12,300 on a $350,000 purchase, but a
  combination of seller concessions, PHFA's forgivable second mortgage, and lender
  credits can take your out-of-pocket cash close to zero.
- **The Roth-vs-prepay question has a real answer, and it flips at ~6.2%.** Below that
  market return, prepaying your mortgage wins. Above it, the Roth wins. This is the
  most useful number in this document.

## 2. How FHA financing actually works in 2026

FHA is the right tool for a first purchase with a thin down payment and a credit score
that is decent rather than excellent. It is not the cheapest loan over 30 years.

| Component | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum down payment | 3.5% | With a 580+ credit score |
| Upfront MIP | 1.75% of base loan | Can be financed into the loan |
| Annual MIP | 0.55% of base loan | For <5% down, loan at/below conforming limit |
| MIP duration, <10% down | **Life of the loan** | Cannot be cancelled by equity alone |
| MIP duration, 10%+ down | **11 years** | Then drops off automatically |
| 2026 FHA floor (most PA counties) | $541,287 | |
| **2026 FHA limit, Bucks & Montgomery** | **$630,200** | 1-unit. 2-unit $806,750 |
| Max purchase price at 3.5% down | ~$653,057 | Because the *loan* cannot exceed $630,200 |

### The single most important FHA trap

Put down **3.5%** and your mortgage insurance runs for **all 30 years**. Put down
**10%** and it stops after **11 years**. On a $350,000 purchase:

| Down payment | Monthly MIP | MIP ends | Total MIP paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5% | $155 | Never (30 yrs) | **$51,975** |
| 10% | $145 | Year 11 | **$19,058** |

That is a **$32,917 saving** for an extra $22,750 at closing. The breakeven is about
year 12, which matters if you plan to stay put. If you expect to move or refinance
within 5–7 years, the 10% down does not pay off — keep the cash and stay flexible.

### Your real exit ramp from MIP

The best long-term play is **not** to pay 10% down. It is to **refinance to a
conventional loan once you reach ~20% equity**, at which point both FHA MIP and
conventional PMI disappear. Appreciation plus principal paydown usually gets you
there in 5–8 years. This is why your rate and your equity position matter more than
your down payment percentage.

## 3. The neighborhoods: what your money actually buys

This is where the "cheap homes" question gets interesting. The three areas are not
variations on a theme — they are different markets with different tax structures.

| | **Upper Black Eddy 18972** | **Harleysville 19438** | **Collegeville 19426** |
|---|---|---|---|
| County | Bucks | Montgomery | Montgomery |
| Effective property tax rate | **0.98%** | 1.36% | 1.45% |
| Tax on a $350k home | ~$3,430/yr | ~$4,760/yr | ~$5,075/yr |
| FHA loan limit (1-unit) | $630,200 | $630,200 | $630,200 |
| Character | Rural, river town, quiet | Suburban, commuter | Suburban, college-adjacent |

**Why the tax gap is so large:** Pennsylvania property tax is the sum of county,
municipal, and school district millage. Palisades School District (Upper Black Eddy)
carries a far lighter millage load than Souderton Area (Harleysville) or the
Perkiomen Valley/Spring-Ford area (Collegeville). The state does **not** reassess at
sale, so the assessed value — and therefore your bill — is largely inherited from
the prior owner.

**The practical consequence:** in Montgomery County, two homes with identical list
prices can have monthly payments that differ by $50–$100 depending on township and
school district. Compare *total monthly cost*, never list price alone.

## 5. How to avoid or eliminate closing costs

Expected closing costs on a $350,000 Harleysville purchase: **$12,302**, plus your
$12,250 down payment.

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Origination / lender fees | $1,689 |
| Underwriting | $1,195 |
| Appraisal | $700 |
| Credit report | $75 |
| Title search + lender's title insurance | $1,750 |
| PA realty transfer tax + recording | $3,750 |
| Tax service / settlement fees | $95 |
| Prepaid property tax escrow (~3 mo) | $1,174 |
| Prepaid homeowners insurance | $1,875 |
| **Total** | **$12,302** |

### Seven ways to drive this toward zero

**1. Seller concessions — the biggest single lever.**
FHA allows the seller to credit up to **6% of the purchase price** toward your
closing costs and prepaids. On a $350,000 home that is **$21,000**, more than enough
to cover the entire bill. In a balanced market you ask; in a slow market you ask for
more. This costs the seller real money, so it works best on homes that have sat for
30+ days or need cosmetic work.

**2. Finance the upfront MIP (1.75%).** This analysis already assumes it. It removes
**$5,911** from your cash-to-close and adds it to the loan. You pay interest on it,
but it preserves cash when cash is your binding constraint.

**3. PHFA Keystone Forgivable in Ten Years (K-FIT).**
**5% of the purchase price with no dollar cap**, as a 0% second mortgage, forgiven
10% per year over 10 years. On a $350,000 home that is **$17,500** — enough to cover
down payment *and* closing costs. Requires a 660+ credit score, liquid assets under
$50,000 after closing, and it must pair with a PHFA Keystone Home Loan first mortgage.
Stay 10 years and you never repay it.

**4. PHFA Keystone Advantage Assistance Loan.** Up to **4% of price or $6,000**,
whichever is less, as a 0% second mortgage repaid monthly over 10 years (about
$50/month). Same 660+ score and asset rules. Cannot be combined with K-FIT — run
both and take the larger.

**5. HOMEstead.** Up to **$10,000**, forgiven 20% per year over 5 years, so fully
forgiven in 5 years. Income-restricted and not available in every county. If you
qualify, this is the most generous structure because the forgiveness is fastest.

**6. Lender credits.** Accept a slightly higher rate in exchange for the lender
paying your closing costs. This is worth it only if you expect to refinance within
a few years — which, given the MIP-elimination strategy in Section 2, you may well do.

**7. Shop the loan, not just the rate.** Freddie Mac's data shows buyers who get
multiple quotes save roughly **$600–$1,200 per year**. Get at least three Loan
Estimates on the *same day* so the rate lock environment is identical.

### The combination that gets you closest to zero cash

> Seller concession (up to 6%) **+** K-FIT or Keystone Advantage second mortgage
> **+** financed upfront MIP **+** documented gift funds if allowed.
>
> Stacked, these routinely cover **100% of down payment and closing costs**. The
> constraint stops being cash and becomes your income-to-debt ratio.

**Do not forget the second mortgage payment.** Keystone Advantage is repaid monthly;
lenders count that ~$50 against your DTI when qualifying you.

## 7. $6,500/year: prepay the mortgage, or fund a Roth IRA?

You asked specifically about putting **$6,500 annually** toward the home payment,
versus investing it in a Roth IRA instead. Here is the honest answer.

### Method — why this comparison is fair

A naive comparison is misleading, because if you prepay you finish early and then
have **no mortgage payment at all** for the remaining years. To make it
apples-to-apples, both paths deploy **the same total dollars** over 30 years:

- **Path A (prepay):** Apply $6,500/yr to principal. The loan is retired early. The
  freed-up payment is then invested at the same assumed return as the Roth.
- **Path B (Roth):** Contribute $6,500/yr for all 30 years.

### Result at $350,000 in Harleysville

| | Prepay mortgage (Path A) | Roth IRA (Path B) |
|---|---|---|
| Loan payoff | **17.8 years** | 30 years |
| Interest saved | **$191,334** | — |
| Balance owed at yr 30 | $0 | $0 |
| Freed cash flow reinvested | $615,026 | — |
| **Total wealth at year 30** | **$615,026** | **$656,975** |
| | | **Roth ahead by $41,949** |

### The answer depends on the market — and the crossover is ~6.2%

| Assumed market return | Path A (prepay) | Path B (Roth) | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4% | $500,728 | $379,134 | **Prepay by $121,594** |
| 5% | $535,712 | $453,445 | **Prepay by $82,267** |
| 6% | $573,717 | $544,711 | **Prepay by $29,006** |
| **6.2%** | ~$592,000 | ~$592,000 | **Tie** |
| 7% | $615,026 | $656,975 | **Roth by $41,949** |
| 8% | $659,950 | $795,248 | **Roth by $135,298** |
| 9% | $708,832 | $965,739 | **Roth by $256,906** |

**Why ~6.2% is the crossover:** prepaying your mortgage earns you exactly your
mortgage rate — **6.162%, guaranteed, risk-free, and tax-free** — because every
dollar of principal you retire stops accruing interest at that rate. The Roth earns
a market return that is volatile and uncertain, but compounds tax-free.

**The decision rule, stated plainly:**

- **If you think long-run market returns will beat ~6.2%** (the historical case, and
  roughly what most planners assume), **fund the Roth IRA first.**
- **If you are risk-averse, or value certainty, or think returns will be muted,**
  **prepay the mortgage** — it is the only option on this page with a guaranteed
  positive return.
- **If you would not actually invest the freed-up cash** after the mortgage is
  retired, prepaying is worse than the table shows, because Path A quietly assumes
  you reinvest. If that money would get spent, the Roth's discipline wins by more.

### Horizon matters if you might move

If you sell or refinance before year 30, the Roth wins by a smaller margin — but it
still wins at a 7% return, and it wins from year one:

| Horizon | Path A | Path B (Roth) | Roth advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 years | $39,028 | $39,996 | $968 |
| 10 years | $92,097 | $96,093 | $3,996 |
| 15 years | $164,258 | $174,772 | $10,514 |
| 20 years | $263,558 | $285,124 | $21,566 |
| 25 years | $407,324 | $439,897 | $32,573 |
| 30 years | $615,026 | $656,975 | $41,949 |

The Roth's edge is **narrow early and wide late** — it is a compounding story, not a
quick win. If you might move in 5 years, the two are nearly identical, and you should
optimize for **flexibility** rather than either one.

### Two caveats on the prepay return

1. **Mortgage interest is only deductible if you itemize.** With the standard
   deduction where it is, many buyers take the standard deduction and get no tax
   benefit from mortgage interest. That does not change the math above, but it means
   do not count on a deduction to improve Path A.
2. **Liquidity.** Money prepaid into a mortgage is locked in home equity. You can
   access it only by selling, refinancing, or a HELOC. Roth contributions (not
   earnings) can be withdrawn penalty-free at any time. For an emergency fund,
   the Roth is strictly more flexible.

### A third option worth knowing: the rate buydown

Instead of either, consider spending the early surplus on **discount points**. At
roughly 3 points (~$10,132 on this loan), the rate drops to about **5.41%**, saving
about **$164/month** and breaking even in **~62 months**. That is a better deal than
prepaying *if* you stay past year 5 and *if* rates do not fall enough to refinance
first.

### Recommended sequencing

1. **Capture the full employer 401(k) match** — an instant, guaranteed return no
   other option here can match.
2. **Build a 3–6 month emergency fund** in cash. Do not skip this to prepay a mortgage.
3. **Fund the Roth IRA.** Tax-free growth plus withdrawal flexibility is worth more
   than the marginal 0.8% expected spread alone suggests.
4. **Then prepay the mortgage** with anything left, treating it as the bond-like,
   guaranteed-return sleeve of your portfolio.
5. **Refinance to conventional at ~20% equity** to kill MIP. This is usually worth
   more than any of the above once you get there.

## 8. Action checklist

**Before you shop**
- [ ] Pull all three credit reports; get every score above 660 (K-FIT threshold)
- [ ] Get pre-approved by **two or three** lenders on the same day
- [ ] Complete a HUD-approved homebuyer education course ($50–$100) — required for PHFA
- [ ] Check PHFA county income and purchase-price limits for Bucks and Montgomery

**When you find a home**
- [ ] Pull the parcel's actual tax bill from the county assessor — never estimate
- [ ] Confirm the school district (boundaries do not follow ZIP codes)
- [ ] File for the **Homestead Exclusion** within 60 days of purchase
- [ ] Confirm the home is FHA-appraisal-ready; older rural homes often are not
- [ ] Ask for seller concessions in the offer, especially on stale listings

**At closing**
- [ ] Compare Loan Estimates line by line across your three lenders
- [ ] Confirm whether upfront MIP is financed or paid in cash
- [ ] Confirm the second-mortgage payment is counted in your DTI

**After closing**
- [ ] Verify your escrow analysis at month 12; taxes commonly adjust
- [ ] Track equity; refinance to conventional at ~20% to eliminate MIP
- [ ] Revisit the prepay-vs-Roth decision annually as rates and returns change

## 9. Caveats and confidence

**High confidence (primary sources):**
- FHA 2026 loan limits — HUD's own `cy2026-forward-limits.txt` dataset
- FHA MIP structure — 1.75% upfront / 0.55% annual
- Today's rates — Fortune / Mortgage Research Center, Sept 10, 2026

**Medium confidence:**
- Effective property tax rates — Census ACS 2019-2023 5-year estimates at ZIP level,
  not parcel-level. Confirm the specific property's assessed value.
- Closing-cost line items — typical for PA, but lender fees vary widely.

**Assumptions, clearly labeled:**
- $1,500/yr homeowners insurance: an estimate, not a quote
- 7% long-run market return: a planning assumption, not a prediction
- No HOA dues, no special assessments, no flood insurance
- No seller concessions assumed in the payment tables (Section 5 shows how to add them)

**Not included and worth your own research:**
- USDA Rural Development eligibility for Upper Black Eddy / Bridgeton Township —
  if eligible, USDA offers **0% down** and often beats FHA on total cost. Verify at
  the [USDA eligibility map](https://eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov/eligibility/welcomeAction.do)
- Current PHFA rate and county income limits, which change
- Whether any specific property will pass an FHA appraisal
